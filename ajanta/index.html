The Ajanta Caves

For seven hundred years, monks and artisans carved prayer halls and monasteries straight into the living rock of a river gorge — then the jungle hid them for more than a thousand more.

CHAPTER 01

The original

Around thirty caves line a horseshoe bend of the Waghur river. The earliest were carved around the 2nd century BCE; a second great wave of work followed in the 5th century CE. Some are viharas, monasteries where monks lived and studied. Others are chaityas, prayer halls built around a stupa.

Their wall paintings — courtly scenes, animals, stories of the Buddha’s past lives — are among the most important surviving examples of ancient Indian painting. Abandoned and overgrown, the caves were brought to wider attention in 1819.

CHAPTER 02

Look closely

  1. 1

    The ribbed ceiling

    The curved stone ribs copy the timber beams of earlier wooden halls — wood construction translated into rock.

  2. 2

    The stupa

    At the far end of the hall stands a stupa, the focus of devotion, with space to walk around it.

  3. 3

    The pillars

    Rows of carved columns divide the hall into a central nave and side aisles for circumambulation.

CHAPTER 03

Shared roots

Ajanta is a Buddhist site, and it stands here deliberately. The museum explores the shared origins of Hinduism, Jainism, Sikhism and Buddhism — traditions that grew from the same soil and still share ideas such as karma, ahimsa and the search for liberation.
